HX‑6 Aurora: A new generation of hatching

HX‑6 Aurora brings together a decade of incubation research and big bets across eggshell‑temperature climate control, robotic egg handling, and welfare‑first chick processing. It sets new marks for hatchability, chick uniformity, energy use, and biosecurity.

Request a demo Read the technical brief
HX‑6 Aurora · Eggshell‑temperature field, rendered

Today we're introducing HX‑6 Aurora, the most capable hatchery system we've ever built. Aurora is a complete single‑stage setter and hatcher line, from egg receipt to chick dispatch, in which every machine shares one climate model and one record of every egg.

Across 14 months of split‑flock pilots at six commercial hatcheries, Aurora hatched 96.8% of fertile eggs, narrowed the hatch window to 18 hours, and used 28% less energy per chick than HX‑5 Meridian. It did this while recording zero cross‑zone contamination events.1

Aurora is available today as a limited preview to existing HX‑5 sites, with general availability in early 2027.

Frontier hatch performance

We measure a hatchery machine the way producers do: chicks per fertile egg, chick quality, how tightly the flock hatches, and what it costs to get there. Aurora leads on every one of them, and it does so on the same eggs, from the same breeder flocks, set on the same day as the machines it is compared against.

The hatch window is the number we care most about. A chick that hatches 30 hours before pull waits that long without feed or water, and it arrives at the farm behind its flockmates and stays behind. Aurora's 18‑hour window means the flock starts together, eats together, and reaches target weight with less spread. Producers in the pilot saw a 0.9‑point improvement in seven‑day uniformity on Aurora flocks.

Single‑stage incubation, re‑engineered

Most setters control air temperature and infer what the embryo is experiencing. Aurora measures it. Every trolley carries a 96‑point infrared array that reads eggshell temperature across the tray stack, and every trolley bay has its own damper, heat‑exchange loop, and humidity injector. The climate model runs at the level of the trolley, not the room.

That matters because a developing embryo is a heat source. By day 12 a full setter is producing kilowatts of metabolic heat, and the eggs in the middle of the stack run warmer than the ones at the edge. Aurora sees the difference and corrects it in minutes, holding the whole load within ±0.05 °C of the program.

Eggshell‑temperature control

Infrared arrays read the shell, not the air. The program is written in eggshell degrees, and the controller holds it to ±0.05 °C across the full load from set to transfer.

CO₂ ramping

Vents stay sealed through early development and open on a programmed ramp to 0.4%, which strengthens the cardiovascular system and shortens the hatch window.

Zone‑by‑zone airflow

Variable‑pitch fans and per‑bay dampers deliver air where the model says heat is building, rather than pushing the same volume past every tray.

Robotic egg handling

Aurora handles every egg from receipt to hatcher without a hand touching it. Candling, in‑ovo sexing, transfer, and in‑ovo vaccination run on one gantry family with shared vision, so an egg that is flagged at candling is remembered at transfer, and nothing is moved twice. The whole day‑18 sequence takes a trolley out of climate for less than four minutes.

Candling and clears removal

Two imaging passes, one in near‑infrared and one in visible light, classify every egg as fertile, clear, or early‑dead. Clears are lifted out and the tray record is updated, so downstream stations only work on eggs that will hatch.

Climate that thinks ahead

Aurora's climate program is not a fixed schedule. It is generated for each set from breeder flock age, egg weight, storage days, and the measured heat curve of the embryos in the cabinet, and it is revised every hour as the eggs report back. When a flock runs hot on day 14, the program cools before the shell does, rather than after.

We trained the embryo heat model on eleven years of setter records and validated it against direct measurement in more than two thousand sets. It predicts eggshell temperature six hours ahead with a mean error of 0.03 °C.

“For twenty years we asked producers to write incubation programs by hand. Aurora reads the eggs and writes the program for them. That is the whole idea.”

Every hatch is logged with its time, so the pull happens when 90% of the basket is dry rather than at a fixed hour. Chicks that hatch early wait in warmth and dim light; chicks that hatch late are not rushed.

Welfare‑first chick processing

Everything after the hatcher is designed around one rule: a chick should be handled as little as possible, and never in a way that would be unacceptable if a person were watching. Aurora replaces the tilting separator with a slow belt and air curtain, counts by vision rather than by drop, and holds chicks in climate‑controlled trays with light, water gel, and airflow until dispatch.

Every chick that leaves an Aurora line is counted, weighed as a tray, and linked to the egg record it came from. Producers receive a per‑flock report with hatch window, uniformity, and any handling exceptions before the truck arrives.

Gentle separation

A low‑speed belt with an air curtain lifts shell debris away from chicks. No tilting, no drops over 8 cm, and no counting by impact.

Vision counting and grading

Overhead cameras count and grade every chick for navel condition, posture, and activity, and flag trays that need a person's attention.

Climate‑controlled holding

Holding trays keep chicks at 33 °C with gentle airflow, dim light, and water gel, so waiting for dispatch costs nothing in early weight gain.

Biosecurity by design

A hatchery is only as clean as its dirtiest hour. Aurora treats biosecurity as an engineering constraint rather than a procedure: eggs, air, people, and waste each move in one direction, through pressure zones that never mix, in machines that can be cleaned in place between every set. In 14 months of pilot operation, Aurora lines recorded zero cross‑zone contamination events and zero positive environmental swabs for Salmonella.

We also changed what the machines will refuse to do. An Aurora hatcher will not open to the chick room while the setter‑side door is unsealed. A transfer head will not enter a cabinet whose last sanitation cycle failed verification. These interlocks cannot be overridden from the operator panel.

  • One‑way flow with pressure cascadeEgg receipt, setter, hatcher, and chick rooms step down in pressure so air only ever moves toward the dirtier zone.
  • HEPA H13 supply per cabinetEvery setter and hatcher filters its own supply air; a failed filter in one bay cannot seed another.
  • Sealed stainless interiors, clean‑in‑placeNo exposed fasteners or seams inside the cabinet. Spray nozzles and a UV‑C cycle run between every set without opening the doors.
  • Continuous ATP and particle monitoringSurface and air readings stream to the site record; a set cannot be started in a cabinet above threshold.
  • Access logging and audit trailEvery door, panel action, and sanitation cycle is logged with who, when, and result, and exported to auditors on request.
  • Third‑party verificationPilot sites were audited by an independent veterinary laboratory each quarter; summary reports are available to producers under evaluation.

Availability

HX‑6 Aurora is available today as a limited preview to existing HX‑5 Meridian sites in Southern Africa. General availability follows in the first quarter of 2027, with Europe and the Middle East in the first half of the year. A retrofit kit brings Aurora's climate controller, sensor arrays, and interlocks to HX‑5 cabinets starting mid‑2027.

Preview sites receive the full setter and hatcher line, the handling gantry family, and Aurora Cloud, with an incubation specialist on site for the first three sets. Pricing is quoted per line and depends on capacity and configuration.
